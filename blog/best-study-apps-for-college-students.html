Best Study Apps for College Students in 2026

Published August 29, 2026 · 7 min read

There are hundreds of "top study apps" lists online, most written by people who've never actually used the apps past the onboarding screen. This one is shorter, more opinionated, and includes what each app is actually bad at — because the wrong app for your workflow is worse than no app at all.

Quick comparison

AppBest forWeakness
NotionAll-in-one notes + task trackingSteep setup time; easy to over-engineer instead of studying
Google CalendarDeadline and schedule trackingNot built for task breakdown, only scheduling
TodoistSimple, fast task listsNo native note-taking; needs pairing with something else
AnkiSpaced-repetition memorization (languages, med/law terms)Unfriendly interface; only useful for memorization-heavy courses
ForestReducing phone distraction while studyingDoesn't help with planning or organization at all

Notion — best if you're willing to set it up properly

Notion can genuinely replace five other apps — notes, task tracking, a reading list, even a habit tracker — but only if you invest an hour or two building a system that fits how you actually work. Most students who "quit" Notion quit because they copied someone else's elaborate template instead of building something simple. Start with one page: a running list of assignments with due dates. Add complexity only when the simple version stops being enough.

Google Calendar — underrated for deadline tracking

It's not exciting, but Google Calendar (or Apple/Outlook Calendar) solves the single biggest time management failure covered in our time management guide: deadlines scattered across too many places. Put every deadline in your calendar the moment you learn about it, set a reminder a few days before, and you've solved most of the problem without installing anything new.

Todoist — for students who find Notion overwhelming

If Notion feels like too much setup, Todoist is the opposite: open it, type a task, set a date, done. It won't hold your notes or readings, but for pure "what do I need to do and by when" tracking, it's faster to actually stick with than a heavier tool.

Anki — only for the right kind of course

Anki's spaced-repetition flashcards are genuinely excellent for memorization-heavy material — vocabulary, anatomy terms, legal definitions, historical dates. It's close to useless for conceptual courses like math proofs or essay writing, where understanding matters more than recall. Don't force it onto every class; use it specifically where memorization is the bottleneck.

Forest — a distraction tool, not a planning tool

Forest (and similar phone-lock apps) address a different problem entirely: staying off your phone during a study session. It doesn't help you plan or track anything, but paired with a real task system (Todoist or Google Calendar), it removes the most common reason a planned study block doesn't actually happen.

So which should you actually pick?

Don't install all five. Pick one task/deadline tool (Todoist if you want speed, Notion if you want everything in one place), keep deadlines in a calendar either way, and add Anki only if you have a memorization-heavy course this semester. The app matters far less than actually using it consistently — see our time management guide for the habit that makes any of these tools stick.
